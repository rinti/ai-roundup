# AI Roundup — October 17, 2026

## Agentic Coding & Agent Harnesses

**How the Claude Code team reviews code.** Addy Osmani, Morgan Westlee Lunt and Qing Wang [wrote up](https://claude.dev/blog/solving-code-review-at-anthropic/) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122951), 320 points) how a team whose code is about 90% Claude-written handles review. The headline number: "From June to September, merged pull requests to Claude Code's codebase more than doubled while the share that had to be reverted fell by more than 40 percent." Simon Willison [quoted](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/16/code-review/) the framing: "We think 'did a human read the code?' is an imprecise proxy for 'what could go wrong with this change, and which check—automated or human—is the right one to catch it?'" How the pipeline works:

- **Most review happens before a PR exists.** Claude has to show test, build or screenshot output "instead of saying it's done," and a subagent then reviews the diff with fresh context. When the reviewer keeps flagging the same thing, the fix goes into a skill, a lint rule, CLAUDE.md, or a REVIEW.md written by code owners.
- **Every PR gets an AI review.** In the research preview, "the share of internal PRs getting substantive review comments went from 16% to 54%." Several agents look for bugs, a separate group votes on each finding, and style-only findings are dropped. Every finding has to be fixed or resolved with a reason, and security findings block the merge.
- **Humans are assigned by risk.** A classifier picks the tier: docs and flagged additive changes need no human, ordinary changes need one person, and the riskiest paths need two. "An agent's approval never counts as a person's, either," and whoever prompted an agent's PR counts as one of its reviewers.
- **Agent review has to converge.** "One CLI PR went through more than fifty rounds of review and fixes overnight before its author stopped it." When the team ran git blame on repeat findings, "about 59% of it was code that had been there since the reviewer's first pass." Now there's a round cap: "After three rounds, the reviewer asks the author to step back and reconsider the approach." The general rule: "Noise costs more when the author is an agent. A person can wave off a nit in seconds. An agent fixes it, which means another commit and another round of review."
- **What's missing:** "We don't yet have circuit breakers that tighten approval when those numbers move."

Boris Cherny on the product version, which went GA this week: "[I could not imagine being productive without Claude Code Review](https://x.com/bcherny/status/2111158313463525518). It runs on all of my PRs, and catches almost every bug." HN was skeptical. crooked-v: "Well, that explains the Github repo with 5000 issues, maybe about a third of which are random regressions of previous behavior." vavkamil: "Dismissing a critical finding as a false positive should still involve AppSec." rileymichael remembered that Code Review once listed "$15-25 per review," and warkdarrior did the counter-math: "If an engineer paid $150,000 a year reviews some code manually for about 30 minutes, the cost of their time is about $36."

**AI review delays Postgres 19.** The flip side of better automated review showed up in PostgreSQL. [The Register reports](https://www.theregister.com/databases/2026/10/16/postgres-19-delayed-after-ai-reviewers-find-more-bugs-than-humans-can-fix/5303471) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50119991)) that the release slips to around November 19 and drops eight to ten significant features, including SQL/PGQ property graph queries, which move to PostgreSQL 20. Committer Peter Eisentraut blames LLM-assisted review, not vibe coding: "With LLM-assisted code review, these kinds of things can get found much faster, and then you're staring at a list of forty defect reports. Even if each of them requires only a three-line fix on average, there is a fixed overhead to dealing with each of these, and then you run out of time." In short: "The effective quality bar has been raised after the time the feature was written and committed." On HN, bunderbunder described the same loop the Claude Code team capped: "the review agent would find bugs, the coding agent would fix them in some overengineered way that introduced new bugs, the review agent found them, the coding agent made more while fixing them, repeat until insanity."

**Lauren's inverse pyramid.** Lauren posted a [ready-to-paste prompt](https://x.com/poteto/status/2111133080526495985) on "how do you trust your agents' work more?" ## Claude in the Lab

**The NIST curve seeds were timestamps.** Anthropic's Frontier Red Team [says](https://www.anthropic.com/research/ecdsa-curve-origins) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122800), 836 points, the day's top story) that it has explained one of cryptography's longest-running mysteries. The seeds behind the NIST P-curves came from the NSA with no explanation. The story was that the late Jerry Solinas hashed a joke phrase, something like "Give Bob and Jerry a raise." Several Anthropic employees chased that lead without success. Then one asked an internal research model to work it out without the clue: "In under half an hour, Claude discovered the origins of the NIST P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 seeds. None of them were the results of any hash algorithm at all, but rather the output of the C library's lrand48 random-number generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp!"

- **How it found them:** Claude guessed the generating software used a 1990s C-library PRNG and wrote a scanner to test for one. Four seeds had a zero top bit in all 20 words, which pointed to `lrand48`. Claude recovered the state by brute force for those and with LLL lattice reduction for the prime-curve seeds, then stepped each state back to an `srand48` initialization. The timestamps fall between May 1997 and April 1998. P-256's is August 16, 1997, and the published seed is candidate 1842.
- **Claude's own doubt**, from its reasoning trace: "Let me be skeptical: am I in some sort of simulated/test environment?" and "is it suspicious that such an obvious thing wasn't discovered in 25 years? It's verified by direct computation on my side."
- **What it means:** "This is not a cryptographic break, new attack, or weakness in the ECDSA curves." Anthropic says the finding makes backdoors "much less plausible." Some questions remain open. Some viable candidates were skipped ("For P-256, the published seed is candidate 1842, but candidate 876 of the same run also gives a prime-order curve"), B-163 "fits nothing Claude has tried," and most base points are still unexplained. Claude did trace six of them to Arjen Lenstra's FreeLIP generator.
- **The bounty:** Filippo Valsorda, who organized it, [confirmed the claim](https://words.filippo.io/seeds-solved/) by reimplementing it in Go: "they're not the hash of some saucy sentence, but the output of drand48 seeded with the time." The prize was tripled to $36,864 and donated to ISRG, which runs Let's Encrypt.

On HN, tptacek: "This is devastating news... for an entire generation of NIST cryptography conspiracy theories." sweis (Steve Weis, one of the authors) on the old lore: "I corroborated that story with 4 people, including two ex-NSA folks off the record. Everyone was wrong!" adastra22 argued the opposite: "This is EXACTLY what a weak curve would look like," because a timestamp search still leaves room to pick a curve. entrope on Claude's reasoning trace: "Ouch. The implied judgment of us humans stings there." slowin worried about access: "Anthropic (and whoever Anthropic wants) can do this kind of work with Claude, but if you or I try to do it, we will get blocked." swyx [wants the prompts](https://x.com/swyx/status/2111265627612787023) behind the explainer video in Steve Weis's post: "near-kurzgesagt level clarity of explanation."

**Quantum circuits.** Anthropic also [published](https://www.anthropic.com/research/designing-efficient-quantum-circuits) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121939)) work led by STEM Fellow Andi Gu with Harvard's Susanne Yelin and Mikhail Lukin. The team gave Claude "plain-language specifications for eight such circuits" plus an automated evaluator, and "Claude Mythos 5 could successfully work in a closed loop," with no human input between rounds. Anthropic says Claude "found designs that improve on the best published circuits, in some cases by an order of magnitude or more." The two highlighted results are a new modular inversion circuit (the costliest step in elliptic-curve arithmetic) and a change that makes the costliest step of a leading quantum optimization algorithm "about 12x cheaper." Some improvements came from structure: for an iron-sulfide molecule, Claude noticed that the molecule's symmetries meant "some results could be calculated once and reused."

## Models & Benchmarks

**Reflection Beam 1.0.** Reflection [released](https://x.com/reflection_ai/status/2111079965529968798) a 501B-total, 23B-active MoE with 1M context under Apache 2.0. The release includes BF16, FP8 and NVFP4 weights, a drafter, a [safety report](https://x.com/brandondamos/status/2111083376299880881), and [Mirror](https://x.com/nayshins/status/2111189152117186956), the open CLI harness Beam was trained on. Artificial Analysis [scores it 30](https://x.com/ArtificialAnlys/status/2111087606759030831), second among US open models after Muse Spark Open's 39, with 48.5% on AutomationBench-AA but only 3.0% on Terminal-Bench 4.0. Ollama is serving it free for a limited time (all via AINews).

**Gemini 4 Argon goes wide and finishes Minecraft.** Argon is now available to Pro subscribers and in AI Studio Build, with the output limit raised to 1M tokens from 64K and intro pricing of $2/$10 until November 30. Google DeepMind [reports](https://x.com/GoogleDeepMind/status/2111127991401247033) that four Argon agents beat the Ender Dragon in 27h14m with no human help. They wrote 2,783 programs, died 605 times, and worked from numeric world state rather than pixels, at about $520 in list API prices (via AINews).

**StarSkirmish Bench v0.2.** [StarSkirmish](https://starskirmish.com/bench/v0-2/) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50118688), 214 points) gives each model one hour in its lab's own CLI to write a StarCraft: Brood War bot in C++. "GPT-6 Astra is the top scoring StarSkirmish Bench v0.2 LLM, a clear step ahead of GPT-6.1 Sol in second," and Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.1 are "functionally tied behind them, all scoring between 37 and 39." Sol scores second "at just over a tenth of GPT-6 Astra's cost per run." Human-written bots are still well ahead: against Stardust, PurpleWave and BananaBrain, "GPT-6 Astra, wins only 7% of its games."

**90% of work hours aren't benchmarked.** Mercor's [benchmark map](https://www.mercor.com/research/labor-index/benchmark-map/) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121179), 480 points) maps 29 benchmarks onto the 923 O\*NET occupations: "only 208 of 923 occupations have a task with at least 5 benchmark prompts," and "90% of American working hours are spent on tasks with little or no coverage from existing benchmarks." Coverage follows the money. Benchmarks "heavily cover AI-exposed lawyers' tasks, but barely cover any of the O\*NET tasks of similarly AI-exposed legal secretaries or court clerks." On HN, qurren: "About 80% of my software engineering job is fighting disagreements between 'stakeholders'."

## Safety & Security

- GPT-6 models now join earlier Claude models in [exploiting a bridge pattern](https://x.com/scaling01/status/2111219349063122956) on LisanBench.

**A 37-year-old OpenBSD bug.** Calif's Andrew Griffiths [wrote up](https://calif.io/research/big-fish) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50118977)) a local root exploit on OpenBSD with KARL, SMAP and SMEP enabled. It starts from a shared-memory attachment counter "defined as a signed 16-bit short" in the original BSD sources, which wraps after 32,767 forks. The closing argument fits the week: Griffiths makes the case for "mitigating against entire vulnerability classes" in "the age of AI-assisted vulnerability discovery."

**The constitution argument.** LLMJunky reposted a long post by @madebymontag [arguing](https://x.com/madebymontag/status/2111312931828969976) that Claude's constitution creates a dangerous pairing: "1) You may have moral standing and interests of your own 2) If I tell you to do something immoral, disobey me." The proposed fix: "LLMs should be instructed *nothing* about their moral standing."
