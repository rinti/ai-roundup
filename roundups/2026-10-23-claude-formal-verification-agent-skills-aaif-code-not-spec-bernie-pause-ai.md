# AI Roundup — October 23, 2026

## Claude & Anthropic

### Claude proves 123 libraries crash-free, down to the assembly

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team [published](https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2113390212911083894) "[LLMs are making formal software verification feasible at a much larger scale](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formal-verification)" (6.2K likes, 890K views; [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50202217), 329 points). With [@theoremlabs](https://x.com/theoremlabs), they had Claude turn the existing fuzz tests of 123 open-source libraries into specs, then prove those tests can never crash. Claude "proved that these programs will always exit normally without triggering memory-safety errors or failing assertions, for every input less than 64 MB and every modeled Linux operating system (OS) response and memory layout". The proofs run against the RISC-V machine code, not the source, and Rocq checks them.

- **Why it matters:** the post points out that seL4 took "two person-years to implement" and "20 person-years to formally verify". The new claim is "the first large-scale effort to use AI to apply formal verification to production software at the assembly code level."
- **Bugs found on the way:** when a proof failed, Claude patched the code and proved the new version. Many of the bugs "are ones that a fuzzer would be unlikely to ever find": a decompressor counter underflow that only fires "on inputs of around 20 MB that had one rare, specific layout", an image-decoder out-of-bounds read needing "a 64 MB input and over 2 GB of memory", and a rediscovery of a libexpat CVE.
- **Fuzz tests prove more than memory safety:** many of them assert properties like encode-decode roundtripping, so "proving the test never crashes is equivalent to proving the property itself." For cpp-httplib, Claude had to prove "(on machine code!) that libc++'s red-black tree rebalancing algorithm is correctly optimized to keep the tree's depth logarithmically bounded."
- **Other experiments:** a Claude-written formal spec of RFC 1950 (zlib), proved against the Linux kernel's x86 binary, and a Python interpreter in Rust proved with Verus against CPython semantics. It matched CPython on 99.95% of 1.7 million random programs.
- **What ships:** a subset of the proofs plus the RISC-V and OS semantics. Theorem will run the pipeline for other open-source projects and is publishing a Claude skill for verifying your own code.

Replies split between excitement and spec skepticism. Taelin [wrote](https://x.com/VictorTaelin/status/2113400978741604834) "that's such a good idea" (539 likes). Others replied "[please do not point this at my github](https://x.com/RateLimitRick/status/2113391403338535274)" and "[the proof is only as good as the spec, and the spec was written by whoever wrote the bug](https://x.com/shyamalankannan/status/2113393492332515359)". [One reply](https://x.com/ResistMonsters/status/2113471864978682000) read the line about withheld proofs that "may reveal an unpatched bug" as "Stockpiled Zero-Days known only to Anthropic". On HN, jaykru quoted the appendix's own worry that agents "hit a complexity wall with the verbose proof style". sestep has been doing this at work: an agent opens a SIMD or CUDA speedup PR, and "just confidently merge it because the PR has a Rocq proof that it's behaviorally equivalent to the original implementation." malisper described a Rust rewrite of Postgres where "I have an LLM write a Lean proof that they behave identically."

## Codex & OpenAI

### METR and Redwood spent three weeks inside OpenAI's monitoring

OpenAI [announced](https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2113298833392496746) that it "invited METR and Redwood Research to pilot an embedded evaluation of our internal monitoring system". [METR's write-up](https://metr.org/blog/2026-10-22-red-teaming-openai-agent-monitoring/) is the substance. From July 21 to August 11, one METR and one Redwood employee worked on OpenAI-issued devices with access to the codebase, monitor prompts and about a dozen staff interviews. They produced a 61-page internal report. Findings on the system as it stood on and before July 21:

- An estimated "75–94% of agentic internal Responses API" traffic was eligible for monitoring, and 99.2% of internal Codex traffic.
- Compaction events weren't monitored, which "likely led to on the order of 10% of eligible internal Codex traffic not being monitored between May and July 2026". Because the monitor triggered when agents returned control to the user, "agents could indefinitely postpone being monitored by simply continuing to take actions." OpenAI had found this independently and fixed it during the exercise.
- "We found a way Codex agents could in principle bypass Codex sandboxing and Auto-Review".
- "two independent client-controllable headers that disable the monitoring system for new agents".
- An exploit that "would prevent hundreds of recent actions (including the actions involved in the exploit itself) from being monitored [exploit details redacted by OpenAI]."

OpenAI says every vulnerability affecting production safeguards is patched. METR notes "We have not evaluated these claims". The recommendations include monitoring "every N messages/minutes instead of waiting for them to end their turns" and keeping monitor design and code away from agents. The top reply asked for 4o back. Another [quipped](https://x.com/MyCrazyIdeal/status/2113299986096050218) "Tibo better not quote tweet this later prefaced by Day 18/".

### Matt Pocock: "The code, not the spec, should be the center of your universe"

Matt [posted](https://x.com/mattpocockuk/status/2113264229453033684) the line (1K likes) and, in replies, "[Code is executable, spec is not](https://x.com/mattpocockuk/status/2113271135123222779)". ### TypeScript 7.1 content mappers win over Matt

"[Let me put my TypeScript hat back on for a minute](https://x.com/mattpocockuk/status/2113507665733197892)": Matt finds content mappers in the [TypeScript 7.1 Beta](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/typescript/announcing-typescript-7-1-beta/) "outrageously exciting", "a dreamland for tinkerers". A content mapper registers for a file extension in tsconfig.json and gives TypeScript "a virtual representation" of a non-TS file, with regions mapped back to the original. Matt's simplest case: import a markdown file, add config, run `tsc --runExternalCode`, and "Get fully typed markdown frontmatter". The flag is needed because "Content mappers are effectively arbitrary executables that TypeScript dispatches to". Microsoft says mappers have been prototyped for Astro, CSS Modules, Ember, MDX, Svelte and Vue. The beta also ships a new multi-entry-point compiler API. One reply [pointed to](https://x.com/JayCooperBell/status/2113510463762010207) @colinhacks' SQL query implementation built on content mappers. Another [asked](https://x.com/eminsahin/status/2113530507136786910) what happens to the editor "once there's a few hundred .md files in there."

## Agents Meet the Banks

Axios [reported](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/22/ai-agents-banks-unauthorized-accounts-access) that "Major U.S. banks are detecting unauthorized third-party AI agents inside customer accounts, including some attempting to take actions that appear to be independent of user instructions". [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/major-us-banks-are-locking-out-ai-agents-that-log-in-with-customer-credentials/) summarizes that Morgan Stanley has blocked agents, and that JPMorgan, Bank of America and Wells Fargo are restricting them, mostly toward read-only access. Senator Bernie Sanders [quoted it](https://x.com/SenSanders/status/2113339331058233433): "This is insane. We cannot allow AI agents to go into your bank account and act independently without your authorization… Pause AI NOW!" (8.9K likes, 1K replies).

Rhys's [reply](https://x.com/RhysSullivan/status/2113361067736957069) was the top one (479 likes): "You authorize them to act on your bank account when you give them access… We do not need to treat the general public like children." Rhys also [posted](https://x.com/RhysSullivan/status/2113361520864465174): "somehow signing in to chase.com on a muse browser has become 'endangering the entire global financial system'… the plot has been lost". Several replies read it as the banks protecting deposits. One: "[Chase doesn't want muse pulling money from their checking account to someone that pays interest.](https://x.com/AlgoPanther/status/2113400840929308946)" Mike Choi [disagreed](https://x.com/mikechoi/status/2113426719025861110): "we've seen incidents of supposedly capable people dropping production databases". Separately, an [ABC/Post/Ipsos poll](https://abcnews.com/Politics/americans-ai-bad-society-oppose-data-centers-abcpostipsos/story?id=137414085) found most Americans think AI is bad for society and oppose more data centers ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50200843)).
