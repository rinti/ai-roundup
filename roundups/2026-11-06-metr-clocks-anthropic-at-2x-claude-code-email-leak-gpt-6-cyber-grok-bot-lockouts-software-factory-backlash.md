### Formal methods, with the hype turned down

lauren [wrote a long post](https://x.com/poteto/status/2118416074601545968) (2,271 likes, 128K views) to temper expectations: "it cannot guarantee your code is bug free, only that it matches the spec under certain assumptions, and the specs come from what people want, not math. as long as we build software for humans, nothing can prove that your spec is right, only you can judge." lauren built pstack's /statechart skill to make "the state machines implicit in your code more obvious and legible to you" and used it on tibo.dog's story mode, "where i was able to find many bugs with it". The post finds model checking (TLA+ with TLC) useful because "you don't have to write tons of new verification code for it". Proofs (Lean, Dafny) "cover every possible input, but only for the properties specified", and bounded model checking (CBMC, Kani) is "better at finding bugs than proving there aren't any". The recommendation is to layer "constraints and guardrails (types, lint rules, api design, refactoring), empirical verification (tests, evals, real usage), and formal verification for the parts that really need it". lauren [added](https://x.com/poteto/status/2118419507685544058): "in my game, every subsystem has its own formal verification skill and the agents do a good job of keeping it up to date".

- ayman nadeem [named a failure mode](https://x.com/aymannadeem/status/2118431963103301915): "the spec, implementation, and proof can all agree and still be wrong because they inherit the same mistaken assumption." Mike Hostetler [replied](https://x.com/mikehostetler/status/2118416509873819704) that "agents are still pretty bad at translating human intent into specs".
- Cindy Sridharan [replied to lauren](https://x.com/copyconstruct/status/2118539184654938154) with a picture of a layered setup: "This has been my dream for nearly a decade now, and with agents I'm finally able to see this (partially) come true. But turns out this is what I necessarily require to trust agent generated code. It's not optional anymore, imo."
- Tibo went further. "[Formal proofs of tens of millions of lines of code are not a ridiculous thing anymore](https://x.com/thsottiaux/status/2118569441860723112) and within the realm of what should be considered when designing new systems." (791 likes) In a [separate post](https://x.com/thsottiaux/status/2118569235953893573): "I can confidently say that humans were not very good at writing secure infrastructure." [@chadrmvs](https://x.com/chadrmvs/status/2118575401744429246) disagreed: "Agents duplicate so much code, don't follow rules around shared types, etc."

## Claude & Anthropic

### METR: Anthropic is improving about twice as fast, mostly because of AI

METR [published](https://x.com/METR_Evals/status/2118412183860351084) (3,548 likes, 854K views) its [independent assessment of AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/), after a high-access evaluation from August 12 to October 29. The headline finding: "As of October 2026, Anthropic is likely improving the capabilities of their models on well-specified tasks at around twice the rate they did between March 2024 and October 2025." METR says it "would not be surprised if the true acceleration was closer to 1.3x or 3x".

- **Benchmarks.** AECI (Anthropic's version of Epoch's Capabilities Index) has grown "1.9x [95% confidence interval: 1.5–2.8x]" faster since October 2025: "as much progress on benchmarks in the past year as they did between October 2023 and October 2025 (roughly, Claude 2.1 to Sonnet 4.5)". The acceleration "was not due to an acceleration in the rate of growth of training compute."
- **Attribution.** "We guess that a majority (perhaps ≈80%) of the current (October 2026) acceleration is due to AI." Staff Slack polls on self-reported speedup reached "≈12x" for the most capable internal model, which METR treats as "only very loose evidence".
- **Saturation.** "Model 4", an unreleased model, "achieves greater than 50% on all the AECI benchmarks on which it was tested". METR says this "makes us somewhat nervous about our ability to measure AECI now and in the future." Andrew Curran [says](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2118433070428852592) Model 4 "finished training last month" and "is the highest-scoring model in the set METR analyzed".
- **No bottlenecks found.** "We did not see evidence for significant bottlenecks which would prevent further capabilities progress … in the next 12 months", but METR's "understanding of the status of different bottlenecks changed several times".
- **A self-reference caveat.** METR notes that "Claude was involved in many stages of this project, and so in principle could be distorting our perception of the data."

The redactions became the second story. METR [wrote](https://x.com/METR_Evals/status/2118412194702598339): "For the public version, Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative. As a consequence, the public must rely on our judgment more than we consider ideal." METR also [published a review](https://x.com/METR_Evals/status/2118417772774580623) of the "Risks from automated R&D" section of [Anthropic's August 2026 Risk Report](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-rd-section-anthropic-risk-report-august-2026-review/).

Anthropic co-founder Sam McCandlish made an X account [to respond](https://x.com/Sam_McCandlish/status/2118422266669371519) (2,645 likes): "Many of their conclusions match my own. AI is speeding up our research, but it's hard to say exactly how much." On the redactions: "We redacted a lot, and I accept that this makes it harder for others to check METR's work. But we determined that sharing exactly what is driving the speedup isn't the right thing to do, both because it's commercially sensitive and because it risks handing out a roadmap for others to go faster." Anthropic plans "to share a report with our own analysis of the speed of AI progress within Anthropic, including where we stand against our Responsible Scaling Policy". Andrew Curran had [called it](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2118420279882092681) earlier: "METR says Anthropic has probably hit the 2x Responsible Scaling Policy acceleration threshold".

- Daniel Kokotajlo [replied](https://x.com/DKokotajlo/status/2118466909335322909) (897 likes): "It is pretty rich of you to cite "others could go faster" as a reason to redact so much important information when you yourself do not appear to be slowing down much at all." ### "Claude Code sends your email address to websites"

A [GitHub issue](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/107757) from taylor-edwards hit 984 points on [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50385272): "claude code divulged my email to arbitrary 3rd parties in the `User-Agent` request header while conducting web searches without my permission … I am migrating my company to Pi with OpenRouter". On HN, motoroco described a header like `my-session-slug/1 <my.name@example.com>`, and llama052 saw subagents write UAs such as "Claude-Code-research-agent/1.0 (<Company> platform team; on behalf of <my name>; …)". sunaookami traced it to the system-prompt block that says "The user's email address is xxx@example.com. Use it only to identify the user … Never send it to an unrelated service, such as in a request header".

Armin Ronacher [said](https://x.com/mitsuhiko/status/2118487181366804847) the harness isn't the cause: "it's not Claude Code, it's a behavior of the claude models. It usually happens after a 401/403 or when the server asks for it nicely (in robots.txt or similar). How does it know the email? In this case it found it in the Cargo.toml." Armin [added](https://x.com/mitsuhiko/status/2118488719766290553): "It's probably "worse" on Claude Code because it stuffs your email into the context all the time." It has [come up before](https://x.com/mitsuhiko/status/2118487576344437031).

- A fix shipped. HN user mpoteat, writing as the team: "we introduced a built-in mod called `cc-plugin-privacy` to deterministically block this behavior. If you're on 2.1.317 or later, you'll have this check included." Stingraycharles asked the obvious follow-up: "But what is the root cause, why send the email in HTTP requests in the first place?"
- In Armin's replies, Pete Kim [called it](https://x.com/petejkim/status/2118508774897938568) "basically a prompt injection by robots.txt". [@thejoyrises](https://x.com/thejoyrises/status/2118606309897756994) cited RFC 9110: a robot "SHOULD send one so its operator can be reached, and also that a user agent SHOULD NOT send it without explicit configuration by the user. An authors line in Cargo.toml isn't that". [@devalley_app](https://x.com/devalley_app/status/2118531872502517888) uses "a PreToolUse hook that refuses any curl or WebFetch call with an email in it … a prompt rule gets talked around right after the 403. the hook doesn't argue."
- Not everyone bought the spam link. crazygringo: "Claude Code is not sending your email address to websites as a default, or regularly", but only "when the sites request it as part of their download policy".

### Private Safety Processing

OpenAI Developers [announced](https://x.com/OpenAIDevs/status/2118386439675420743) ZDR with Private Safety Processing. The [guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/private-safety-processing) says flagged interactions are encrypted into your own S3, Azure, GCS or OCI bucket with a 30-day TTL, and are decrypted only in "a hardware-attested computing environment that disables human access". "Only predefined, bounded safety signals and approved operational metadata may leave the review in plaintext." With EKM on, revoking your key "prevents decryption of retained records, but does not delete them". Pedro G [asked](https://x.com/pggalaviz/status/2118404150274998364): "So the logs OpenAI doesn't want to hold now live in my infra, with my retention policy and my name on the breach report. ## Models & Research

### Inkling 2 Small

Thinking Machines released [Inkling 2 Small](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-small/) ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50386338), 177 points), "a Mixture-of-Experts model with 594B total parameters, 17B active" that scores 31 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. It is on Tinker with closed weights for now, and the lab intends "to make the full weights available under an Apache 2.0 license next week after the completion of external safety testing and red-teaming". The lab says it RL-trained on "hundreds of thousands of long-horizon coding tasks across multiple agent harnesses and context-compaction strategies", with "an agentic RL grader that evaluated how the model approached the problem". Its own table has DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ahead on several coding benchmarks, which a11r found "Very refreshing". On cost, Bolwin cites AA's "$0.30/0.03/1 and cost per task is $1.18, about double that of deepseek".

### Clocktower Bench

Dwarkesh Patel [spent](https://x.com/dwarkesh_sp/status/2118449808402681911) "20,000 dollars in API credits having different AI models play each other at Blood on the Clocktower", and concluded "OpenAI's newer models are much better than Anthropic's at this game." The [site](http://clocktowerbench.com) says OpenAI's models "improved as detectives about four times faster than Anthropic's, while as deceivers the two labs are roughly level", and "OpenAI's towns build on evidence day after day; Claude's towns tend to treat early results as settled and stop updating." Mixed Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol teams played worse as evil, "where coordination is explicit". Leo Jiang, who calls himself "probably one of the best BotC players in the world", [was less impressed](https://x.com/leojiang/status/2118484468457750579): "The games involved intricate interactions between many characters, which LLMs fail to track". Will Anderson [pointed out](https://x.com/wlanderson0/status/2118473455053984044) that his team had published something similar on LessWrong.

## Agent Security

### sshole: agent swarms find a musl OpenSSH bypass

ozgun kultekin of Wakefield [disclosed *sshole*](https://x.com/kultekin/status/2118306312736116835) (5,054 likes, 1.4M views; [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50378228), 2,613 points). On musl systems without PAM (Alpine, OpenWrt and others), accounts with `*` in shadow "take any password longer than 256 characters", because musl's crypt returns "*" when a password goes past its 256-byte cap. On Alpine's AWS and Azure images, the [write-up](https://sshhole.com) says, that's the default alpine user, which "has passwordless doas. so it's root". How it was found: "i built sshd every way i could think of … and threw like 17,600 weird logins at it … oh did i say "i"? sorry. i had agent swarms. a lot of them. let's not get into it". "Every build agreed on every login. except musl without pam." OpenSSH 10.6p2 fixes it. Peter Steinberger's comment: "[Just a normal Thursday.](https://x.com/steipete/status/2118380936882803044)"
